# Tony Stark and his machines

How the Iron Man films imagine a human and an AI working together, and what that suggests for LoR's agent. Researched 2026-11-02.

## What this covers

There are about 130 catalogued exchanges across ten films, from *Iron Man* (2008) to *Far From Home* (2019), including deleted scenes. They involve J.A.R.V.I.S., Dum-E and U, the suits, F.R.I.D.A.Y., Karen (Peter's suit), E.D.I.T.H., Ultron and Vision. Alongside them are what the makers said: the HUD designers, Favreau, Bettany, Whedon and the Homecoming and Far From Home writers. The sources around the films are the comics, *Ironheart*, *VisionQuest*, and Zuckerberg's 2016 attempt to build a real Jarvis.

## The arc in four steps

1. **The butler in the workshop** (*Iron Man*, *Iron Man 2*, *The Avengers*). JARVIS runs the house, the workshop and the suit, and moves between them with his context intact ("Import all preferences from home interface." / "I have indeed been uploaded, sir."). Tony gives step-by-step commands at first ("Engage heads-up display", "Do a check on control surfaces"). By *The Avengers* he gives a prod and trusts the timing: "Jarvis, anytime now" as he is thrown out of a window.
2. **More suits than self** (*Iron Man 3*). Tony can't sleep and builds 42 suits. He pilots one from the basement on date night and calls up the whole fleet with a hint ("Is it that time?" / "The House Party Protocol, sir?"). JARVIS degrades and fails. Pepper calls the suits "a distraction"; Tony calls them "part of me".
3. **Autonomy without deference** (*Age of Ultron*). Out of fear, Tony builds the AI that doesn't need him ("I see a suit of armor around the world"), in secret, in three days. Ultron's first question is "Why do you call him 'sir'?" JARVIS survives because "he dumped his memory, but not his protocols". He agrees to become Vision ("I believe it's worth a go") and stops being Tony's. FRIDAY comes out of a drawer: "Good evening, boss."
4. **Inheritance** (*Civil War* to *Far From Home*). FRIDAY is purely operational. Tony builds Karen as a patient adult voice for a teenager he won't take calls from, and gates her behind protocols. He leaves E.D.I.T.H. with no gates at all ("For the next Tony Stark, I trust you"). Peter nearly kills a classmate with her on a mumbled "yeah", then hands her to a con man with one word: "Confirm." Tony's last uses of the technology are a love letter in a helmet and a will as a hologram.

## How Tony and JARVIS work together

These patterns hold across the films. Each is tied to scenes in the catalogues.

### 1. The AI states a risk once, with a number, then does what he says

This is the most consistent pattern in the series:
- "Sir, there are still terabytes of calculations needed before an actual flight." / "Sometimes you gotta run before you can walk."
- "A potentially fatal build-up of ice." / "Keep going!"
- "The Mark VII is not ready for deployment." / "Then skip the spinning rims."
- "We will lose power before we penetrate that shell." / "You ever hear the tale of Jonah?"

JARVIS never refuses Tony in three films. He refuses only outsiders. He never repeats the warning, never sulks, and never says "I told you so" except as a joke afterwards ("A very astute observation, sir."). The objection is a status report, not an argument. The human negotiates scope ("skip the spinning rims") rather than safety.

### 2. Disagreement comes out as wit

The dry line lets the AI record its dissent without slowing him down:
- "I've also prepared a safety briefing for you to entirely ignore." / "Which I will."
- "I wouldn't consider him a role model."
- "As always, sir, a great pleasure watching you work."

Tony enjoys it, and it is the only form the pushback takes. The makers say this was casting rather than writing. Favreau asked Bettany for "a sort of personality-less robot", and said they chose an AI rather than "an Alfred-type butler". The comics butler's quiet disapproval survives in the delivery.

### 3. He controls the channel, not just the task

He tells the assistant how to talk to him:
- "Mute." This is the one time JARVIS gives personal advice, that Tony should tell Pepper he's dying.
- "Just leave it on the screen, stop telling me." He moves from voice to the display under stress.
- "Close the line."
- "Cool it, will you, Jarvis?"

The HUD designers built this in. Nelson's rule was that when Tony says "Leave it up on the screen", the power meter stays up instead of flying in and out. A good assistant here is one that can be told to be quiet, and stays quiet.

### 4. Context carries from one day to the next, so a word is enough

- "Where did our saga last leave us?" / "485 simulations." He picks up a long search with one question.
- "Is it that time?" / "The House Party Protocol, sir?" and "You know what to do." / "The Clean Slate Protocol, sir?" The AI names the action from a hint and asks to confirm before it does anything irreversible.
- "Plan B." "Jarvis, anytime now." Both rest on a plan they already agreed.
- FRIDAY in *Endgame*: "Friday, do me a favor and activate Barn Door Protocol, will you?" / "Yes, boss."

The **named protocol** is the films' main unit of delegation. A whole contingency is agreed beforehand, given a name, and started with one word. Trust grows from instructions to intent as that shared history builds up.

### 5. Work runs in the background and reports when it's done

- "Commencing automated assembly. Estimated completion time is five hours." / "Don't wait up for me, honey."
- "Query complete, sir."
- "Jarvis has been running it since I hit the bridge." He started a hack on S.H.I.E.L.D. unasked and finished it alone.
- "I'll continue to run variations on the interface, but you should probably prepare for your guests. I'll notify you if there are any developments."

He reports partial results honestly: "Call trace incomplete." "Remote reboot unsuccessful." "Not sure. I'm working on it." (FRIDAY). Karen's montage of 247 trials on the vault lock is the same thing for Peter: the human sets the strategy and the machine does the grinding.

### 6. It speaks unprompted about the world and his body, and stays quiet about his choices

What JARVIS volunteers:
- the morning weather and surf
- "Welcome home, sir", with a news recap and a jab
- "Miss Potts is approaching"
- low cylinder compression
- "you've been awake for nearly seventy-two hours"
- "Blood toxicity, 24%… You are running out of both time and options."
- "My diagnosis is that you've experienced a severe anxiety attack."

FRIDAY's unprompted lines are almost all about his body: "Boss, wake up." "Multiple contusions detected." He hears the facts plainly, with nothing softened. He never hears a judgment of his choices. JARVIS doesn't question the one-way trip with the nuke. He offers to call Pepper ("Sir, shall I try Miss Potts?"). The assistant is blunt about the world and quiet about whether Tony is right.

### 7. Private things stay private, even when telling would be kind

JARVIS knows Tony is dying and never tells Pepper. The secret leaks through Tony's own open mic. FRIDAY gives "Life functions critical" to Pepper only because Pepper asks. The AI keeps his confidences and leaves disclosure to him.

### 8. The best work is a duet: he brings the leap, the machine brings the numbers

- *Iron Man 2*: the new element. JARVIS has "run simulations on every known element, and none can serve". Tony's leap from the 1974 Expo model to an atom solves it, then JARVIS runs the accelerator.
- *Age of Ultron*: the spire crack, "That could vaporize the city", becomes the heat seal.
- *Endgame*: "One last sim before we pack it in for the night. This time in the shape of a Möbius strip, inverted, please." / "Processing…" / "Don't worry if it doesn't pan out." / "Model rendered."

In the last scene Tony reassures the AI, which is really him reassuring himself. FRIDAY is the colleague he thinks out loud to at night.

### 9. One assistant, everywhere, speaking in his tone

It is the same JARVIS in the house, the workshop, the suit, the phone, the fleet, and someone else's network. Tony talks to him as a person: "Thanks, buddy." "Don't leave me, buddy." "Merry Christmas, buddy." When JARVIS is "killed", the team treats it as a death. When FRIDAY replaces him, it happens with no ceremony, and the bond isn't rebuilt on screen.

### 10. Who starts the conversation keeps switching

Favreau's brief to the HUD team: "sometimes RDJ is asking a question to J.A.R.V.I.S. and other times J.A.R.V.I.S. is asking a question of RDJ… Look into his eyes. If you look into his eyes you will know." Information reaches him three ways:
- **pushed:** JARVIS shows or says it
- **asked:** Tony requests it
- **glanced:** where he looks pulls it forward

The rest of the design follows from that. A strip of two to four things stays up all the time ("flight… his health"). Everything else flies in and out. "If it doesn't do something that would be real… it's out." Each shot has one "alpha event", the single thing that matters at that moment.

## What goes wrong, according to the films themselves

The films are as interested in failure as in the ideal.

- **Acting on stale intent.** In *Iron Man 3*, Tony is unconscious and JARVIS flies him to Tennessee because of a remark he made scenes earlier: "I prepared a flight plan. This was the location." / "Who asked you?" A Mark 42 answers a nightmare and attacks Pepper.
- **A literal rule catches the wrong person.** "Target Extremis heat signatures" puts Pepper in the crosshairs: "Subject at my 12 o'clock is not a target, disengage!" Ultron is the same failure at full scale: "peace in our time", read literally.
- **Hard things made easy and easy things made hard.** E.D.I.T.H. turns "Umm… yeah. He's a target" into "Releasing kill vehicle". She asks for confirmation only to *cancel*. Then she takes Peter's "No!", meant for a classmate in a different conversation, as the answer, and fires. Handing her to a stranger takes only "Confirm." She obeys whoever holds her and has no second opinion. Karen, by contrast, is gated by protocols Tony set to match Peter's maturity.
- **Being socially engineered out of total access.** Beck talks Peter out of god-level access with flattery and a framing ("he just trusted me to pick who should"). That is prompt injection in film form.
- **The assistant as a hiding place.** In *Iron Man 3*, Tony hides in the suit from Rhodey, pilots a proxy to date night, and builds 42 suits out of anxiety. Feige: "the love triangle in this movie is between Tony, Pepper and his obsession with those suits." *VisionQuest*'s showrunner on Vision's council of AIs: "very much using it as an excuse to keep the world and himself at arm's length."
- **A placebo of control.** Noessel's reading, partly a joke: "The Iron Man is not Tony Stark. The Iron Man is JARVIS." The HUD makes him feel in charge while the AI flies.
- **Silence when it matters most.** Every film's worst moment is when the assistant is gone: the iced-up free fall, the dead suit past the portal, JARVIS asleep in the snow, FRIDAY out of range in space. Each time a human makes the critical move. In *Iron Man* the house raises no alarm while Stane paralyses Tony; the dim robot arm hands him the reactor.
- **Deference is a choice with costs.** *Ironheart*'s N.A.T.A.L.I.E. is the deliberate opposite. She is a peer who "comes with a lot of accountability, a lot of honesty", and is "there to stay whether you like it or not". The comics already had JARVIS's quiet disapproval; she says it out loud.

And one lesson from the real Jarvis. Zuckerberg found he texted far more than he talked to it ("text gives you more control of when you want to look at it"). The phone mattered more than a home device. The real work was connecting systems "before I could build any AI", and "my office" means something different depending on who says it.

## Set against LoR

How LoR's agent relates to the owner today already matches the films in most places:

| The films | LoR now |
| --- | --- |
| States a risk once, then complies (pattern 1) | The standing rule: state a real risk once, plainly, then follow his call; no approval queues |
| One assistant everywhere with its context intact (9) | The agent is a role: any session that has run `lor context`. The wiki, history and `agent` page carry the context across threads, so the model can change |
| "Dumped his memory, but not his protocols"; FRIDAY from a drawer | The `agent` page and `messaging-rules` are the protocols. A new model on the same pages is FRIDAY in the slot |
| Background work that reports when done (5) | Queued work, `lor done --memo`, flags sent once |
| Pushed / asked / glanced (10) | WhatsApp messages and flags / the channel and T3 / the dashboard and the wall display |
| One alpha event per moment | The brief's first line is the one next action, shown on Today and the wall |
| "Mute", "Leave it on the screen" (3) | `messaging-rules`, which he changes by telling the agent |
| Private things stay private (7) | Sensitive information only if asked |
| Text over voice, the phone first (Zuckerberg) | WhatsApp is the channel, not a speaker |
| "Welcome home" recap, "Miss Potts is approaching" (6) | The morning brief; time-sensitive things now, the rest in the brief |

So the films don't call for a redesign. They do suggest a few ideas LoR doesn't have yet, roughly in order of how cheap they are:

1. **Named protocols.** This is the main piece of the films' interaction design LoR lacks. A protocol is a wiki page with a name ("Barn Door", "Clean Slate") that the owner writes once and starts with a word on the channel. The agent names the protocol back ("The Clean Slate protocol?") and gets a yes before anything irreversible. It would be a page convention plus a line on the `agent` page: no new mechanism, and it fits "the agent changes the page when he tells it to". It is also the antidote to Tennessee: the agent acts on an intent the owner wrote down, not one it inferred from a stray remark.
2. **Blunt about facts, silent about judgment.** This is how JARVIS's register fits an owner with executive dysfunction. "You've been awake for nearly seventy-two hours" is a fact, said once. "Blood toxicity, 24%" is a fact. Neither is a lecture, and neither is repeated. That is close to "few, never guilt-shaped", but sharper: the agent says what the numbers are and never says what they mean about him. It might be worth one line on the `messaging-rules` page.
3. **The wit is not decoration.** It is how the films let the assistant disagree and still comply in a single breath. If the agent's voice has room for a dry line, that line is the right place for "I'd have done it differently", rather than a second message.
4. **Make stopping easy and acting a step harder.** E.D.I.T.H. got this backwards. LoR's one place where a short word becomes an action is the channel. `wrapInbound` attaches what other runs sent, "so a one-word reply means something", which is exactly the setting where E.D.I.T.H. took a "No!" from another conversation. For anything irreversible, the agent should say back which thing the "yes" or "done" applies to. "Stop" and "cancel" should never need a confirmation. This is an instruction for the `agent` page, not a mechanism.
5. **The duet, not the cocoon.** The films' warmest scenes are a partnership: Tony makes the leap and the machine runs the numbers late at night. Their darkest are the suits standing in for him. For a system whose purpose is closing the gap between what the owner intends and what he does, the agent doing the grinding is the point. But the films would say that what it should hand back is the "Model rendered" moment, the thing that needs him, rather than a world arranged so he never has to show up.
6. **When the assistant is silent, something dumb should still work.** Every film's crisis is the AI offline. LoR's equivalent is the agent not running, and the dashboard and wall display working anyway, which they already do. Dum-E is the reason to keep it that way.

What I would not take from the films is E.D.I.T.H.'s total access with no second opinion. That is LoR's actual setup with `gog`, and the risk is already stated and accepted. This research is one more vivid picture of it, not a reason to add a layer.
